About Green Paws

Green Paws is an intellectual property brand that features four dogs who have adventures in Brooklyn Bridge Park. Each dog senses the world differently and they work together to solve problems using STEM concepts. Together, this shows the scientific method in an entertaining and fun way, taught to children aged three to five without ever using those words. The first product is a book entitled Green Paws: The Missing Bees.

What is underneath: how the Pack thinks

The four dogs are not just four personalities. Together they are a small model of how to think well, and how to form good judgment. Each dog is one move in careful thinking:

  • Maxi looks closely. She gathers what is really there. But she learns that being correct is not the same as solving the problem, evidence alone is not judgment.
  • Angus acts and tests. He tries things, nose first. He learns to pair his instinct with what the others notice.
  • Zuri listens differently. She takes in what the others miss. Good judgment needs the perspective no one else has.
  • Winston brings it together. He weighs everyone’s piece and helps the Pack decide.

This is the quiet heart of the science in Green Paws. It is not facts to memorize. It is a way of thinking: look closely, gather what you notice, weigh it, hear how others see it, then decide together. The story shows, again and again, that no single dog is right alone. Good judgment comes from combining them. A child never hears the words “scientific method” or “judgment.” They just watch the Pack think, and slowly learn to do it too.

Two lenses, one idea. The four dogs above show how the Pack thinks together (observe, act, listen, decide). Later, the tagline (Sniff, Wag, Bark) shows the same spirit as one child’s simple loop: notice, delight, share. The four-part model is the Pack’s teamwork; the three-part tagline is a single child’s experience. They are two views of the same curious, caring way of meeting the world, not competing lists.

Positioning: how Green Paws stands apart

Green Paws reveals the magic of nature close to home, so children grow to love and care for the world outside their door.

What sets Green Paws apart is that its world is real. Other beloved kids’ brands are set in invented places. Green Paws is set in a real, named park a child can actually walk into. The magic they discover in a book is waiting for them at the very park in the story, and at their own park down the road.

Three things only Green Paws has together

  • Real, not invented. A real park children can visit, not a world that exists only in a book.
  • A park: shared and public. Free, open, and for everyone, the outdoors that already belongs to every child.
  • Close to home, not faraway. The wonder is the ordinary local park, not exotic creatures in distant places.

Franchise rule: every Green Paws story is set in a real park. Always. The early books deepen one real place, Brooklyn Bridge Park, and in time the Pack may travel to other real parks. However far it goes, the whole collection is an invitation to go outside and look closely, wherever a child lives.

Green Paws Book Business Objective

This first book is not for sale. It is proof that the property is ready to become a brand.

Green Paws is a series

Green Paws is designed as a series. The four dogs, Winston, Maxi, Angus, and Zuri, are the permanent Pack, and they appear in every book. The first book is The Missing Bees. The early books are all set in Brooklyn Bridge Park, deepening one real place the way a child comes to know their own park, closely, over many visits. In time, the Pack may travel to other real parks. Recurring characters like Robin appear across books, starring in some and playing smaller roles in others. The world is built to grow, always in a real park.

It is a proof of concept: a small print run that shows Green Paws is ready to become something much larger, a licensing business. So the book has one job, to make everyone who holds it believe the property is real and ready. It must prove three things:

  • The four dogs work as one designed family.
  • The world is iconic and can be licensed.
  • The whole thing was created with a clear vision, not just assembled from pieces.

Anyone who sees it, a licensing partner, a writer, or a shop, should feel one thing: this is not just a book. It is a brand, and the book is only the first part. So every element we design could become a future product, and each piece must be simple, iconic, and clear at any size, from a book page to a small toy tag.

Brooklyn Bridge Park Setting

A real, shared, city park. The wonder is already there.

Brooklyn Bridge Park is the perfect place for this story, and not by accident. It is a real, working waterfront park that is also beautiful, and it belongs to everyone: every background, every ability, every walk of life.

The dogs are magical characters, and the park is real. The wonder in the park is real too, and it is what matters most. The wonder was already in the park. The dogs do not add it; they only help a child see it. The magic comes from the real world, when you look closely.

Green Paws may travel to other parks in future stories, but every book is always set in a real park, never an invented one, and it never becomes an epic nature adventure. New places are explored the same way as home: up close, at a child’s eye level, hosted by the locals who know them. The wonder is always in looking closely at a real place with the people who love it, not in the spectacle of somewhere far away. Every book is intimate, wherever it is set.

The ordinary outdoors, when you pay attention, is extraordinary.

In the spirit of The Snowy Day

Sixty years ago, Ezra Jack Keats showed that the magic of the outdoors lives in the city. A snowy street is as full of wonder as any wild forest, and every child deserves to see themselves in it. Green Paws carries that idea forward.

Brand Overview

Four layers that connect: vision, mission, promise, and a tagline for kids.
Vision — the world we want
Every child knows the magic of nature is theirs to love and care for.
Mission — how we get there
Inspire children to look closely at nature close to home, have fun with it, and care for it.

Looking closely and getting data from what you see, hear, smell, taste, or touch is the root of early science thinking (STEM) and critical thinking. Caring for it is the root of stewardship. The fun is what makes it stick. A child who enjoys looking closely and caring for a park will continue to do both.

Brand promise — for adults
Outside is all ours.
Tagline — for kids
Sniff. Wag. Bark.

How the tagline carries the mission

For young children, early science thinking is not equations. It is the scientific method: observe, investigate, share.

  • Sniff = observe and investigate. Notice, follow the clue, pay attention.
  • Wag = delight, fall in love. The joy of discovery.
  • Bark = share, speak up. Tell the Pack, care enough to protect it. This is stewardship.

Brand Personality

Who Green Paws is. The source for the voice, the pictures, the pages, and the type.

The Green Paws brand personality is the source for everything: the voice, the pictures, the page layouts, and the type. The feeling is intimate. And intimate does not mean small. What is close, the love, the worry, the truth between characters, is of the highest importance. A missing bee can matter as much as a big problem. We hold the small thing close and let it carry real stakes.

The five words that define the personality

Warm not sweet
Real feelings are allowed. Robin truly grieves. Maxi truly doubts herself. (More on this below.)
Sincere not ironic
Everyone cares about small things, completely. The humor comes from that sincerity, never from mocking it.
Generous not competitive
The park belongs to everyone. Nobody wins by beating anyone.
Curious not knowing
The dogs notice, they do not explain. The book asks more than it tells, and never lectures.
Rooted not generic
The setting is a real park. The magic is in the real world, not a fantasy land.

Things Green Paws would never do

✕ Be mean, or make a joke at a character’s expense
✕ Wink at the adult over the child’s head
✕ Be placeless: a nowhere setting, a generic park
✕ Talk down to a child, or turn wonder into a lesson
✕ Shout, crowd the page, or perform at the reader

Warm, funny, and sweet are different things

Warm and funny are not the same thing, and they are not equal. Every page must be warm. Not every page must be funny. The saddest, quietest pages (like Maxi’s doubt, or the big page 22) are not funny at all, and that is right. Warmth is always there. Humor is added when it fits.

Warm and funny kids’ brands

  • Bluey. Genuinely warm, real family moments, episodes that make parents cry, and genuinely funny, the humor from the kids being sincere and the dad being a bit ridiculous, never from snark or cruelty. Quiet, domestic, no villains, no spectacle, and the biggest kids’ property in the world.
  • Winnie the Pooh. Warm to its core, funny because Pooh takes small things (honey) with total seriousness and Piglet worries about everything. The humor comes entirely from sincerity.
  • Paddington. A polite bear who causes chaos out of pure good intentions. Physical, innocent comedy; nobody is ever the butt of a mean joke. The underlying emotion is about abandonment and loss.
  • Frog and Toad. Gently, quietly funny (Toad’s cookies, his lost button), and deeply warm about friendship. Less madcap than the others.
  • Elephant and Piggie (Mo Willems). The modern picture-book master of it. Laugh-out-loud funny for a four-year-old, and warm about friendship underneath every joke.
  • Toy Story. Real stakes and real feeling, comedy that comes from character, warmth that earns the tears.

When you are unsure if a choice is right, ask one question: does this feel like a warm person, kneeling down to show a child something small and real, with care and no irony? If Nickelodeon is the cool kid who thinks adults are silly, Green Paws is the preschool child who kneels in the grass and says “wait, look at this”, and means it.

Warm — our foundation ✓

Means: lets a real feeling exist, then meets it with care.

You see it in: The Little Prince, The Snowy Day, Charlotte’s Web, Frog and Toad.

In our book: Maxi promises “we’ll find them,” then doubts, “what if we can’t?” The worry is real before it is comforted.

Zany — our humor ✓

Means: warm, innocent, silly comedy that comes from sincerity, never cruelty.

You see it in: The Monkees, Curious George, Paddington, Amelia Bedelia, Elephant and Piggie.

In our book: Robin attacks an ice cream truck with a tiny fork. Winston treats one missing bee like a naval crisis.

Sweet — never us ✕

Means: removes all the stakes to keep everything soft and nice.

You see it in: Care Bears, CoComelon, Precious Moments.

The sweet version of our book: the bees appear on page 3, nobody worries, everyone is happy the whole time. Pleasant, and forgotten by dinner.

Green Paws is a quiet brand

Not shy, not boring. Quiet means it leans in close instead of shouting, and draws the child near instead of performing. It belongs with The Little Prince, The Snowy Day, Frog and Toad, and Winnie the Pooh: the properties that are loved slowly and passed down for generations. Quiet does not mean sweet, Kevin Henkes is quiet and a little sweet; Green Paws is quiet but keeps its real feelings and its edges.

How a quiet brand is sold (this shapes everything)

  • Sold to the adult who loves the child — the thoughtful parent, grandparent, teacher, or librarian. Our whole look is built to win that careful, loving choice.
  • Earned, not bought — grows through reviews, awards, gift guides, bookshops, libraries, and word of mouth. One trusted recommendation beats a hundred ads. We will build advisory boards with two kinds of gatekeeper: reading gatekeepers (librarians, teachers, parents) and place gatekeepers (local parks commissioners, conservancies, Friends-of-the-park groups).
  • The marketing feels like the book — warm, sincere, calm, never loud. If a piece of marketing sounds like shouting, it is wrong. It should feel warm and funny where appropriate.
  • It grows over time — the goal is to become the book families quietly agree is the special one, and pass down.

Designing the Page

For the illustrator. How to arrange each page so it feels like Green Paws.
Every rule comes from one idea: the page should reward a child for looking closely.

Keep the camera low and close

Imagine a small camera down in the grass, at the height of a dog’s eyes. That is the crouch: kneeling down to look at a small thing. It is the heart of the brand.

  • Keep horizons low, so we feel close to the ground.
  • Get close. Do not show every scene from far away.
  • Make the small thing big. When a dog studies a bee, let it be large. The child should see what the dog sees.

Rich world, one clear focus

Here is the rule that guides every page, and it is easy to get wrong in two opposite ways. The page should be rich, but never busy: a full, living world, with one clear thing the eye goes to first.

  • Too empty is wrong. One dog floating in a blank space looks cheap and unfinished. “Give it room” does NOT mean leave the page bare.
  • Too busy is wrong. A hundred things all the same size, all shouting, gives the eye nowhere to land. The story gets lost.

The target is in the middle: a rich page, full of the real park, where one thing clearly leads. You make it lead not by removing everything else, but by giving the main thing more size, more light, and a little breathing room around it. The world can be as detailed as you like, as long as the eye knows where to go first.

Page 10, three ways. Left: too empty, looks unfinished. Middle: rich world, one clear focus — the target. Right: too busy, the story is lost. The middle and right have almost the same number of things; the middle just gives Maxi size and light so she leads.

  • Choose one focus per page. What is the single most important thing here? Make it the biggest, or put the light on it.
  • Give the focus breathing room, relative to everything else. That is what “space” means: room around the main thing, not an empty background.
  • Example, page 10: Maxi finds the flowers are old. The page is full — wilted flowers, boulders, the fountain, Zuri hidden as a rock, Angus and Robin to the side — but Maxi is largest and lit, so the eye lands on her first.

Two kinds of page

Both kinds follow the rule above. The only difference is how many things the child looks at.

Story pages (most of the book): one focus

These pages carry the story, so the eye must go to one place. One clear focus, a rich world around it, arranged so the important thing leads.

Discovery pages (endpapers, a few big park scenes): a few things to find

These pages are made for exploring, so you may hide several small stories. But they are still calm, never a hundred things shouting.

  • Reward attention with depth, not volume. Fewer discoveries, deeper ones. A pile of a hundred toys is exciting for a minute, then forgotten; one gift, opened slowly, is remembered.
  • Each hidden thing is a little story, not just an object. A child lost a balloon, and it is caught in a tree nearby.
  • Keep hidden things clearly drawn. “Hidden” means tucked in a findable place, not faint. The joy is in successfully finding it.
  • Give each discovery its own quiet space. The calm around a hidden thing is like wrapping paper: it makes finding it feel special.

Build every page for two ages at once

Age 3
Big characters and physical comedy. They love Robin, and they laugh that the dogs think the ice cream truck is a giant flower.
4–5
The feelings and the first discoveries. Maxi’s doubt, Robin’s sadness under the comedy, the start of Find Zuri.
6+
The connections. A small detail in one book that becomes important in the next.

The rule for every page: give the youngest child something complete on the surface, and reward the older child with something underneath. Nothing important should be hidden so deeply that the youngest child feels they are missing something.

Match the light and mood to the feeling

Hope and calm: bright, clear spring morning light, with soft shadows.

Worry and doubt (pages 15–20): a little heavier and quieter, so the reader feels the struggle. Not dark or scary, just less bright.

Relief and joy (pages 25–26): light returns, warm and open. Zuri’s first smile should feel like sunrise.

The one big page (pages 21–22)

One page has very large words: “WHERE COULD THOSE BUZZING BEAUTIES BE?” Handle it with care.

It is a sigh, not a shout. Warm, gentle, wondering letters. Not loud or sharp. A soft, sad, hopeful feeling, made big.

The type is the picture. Lots of soft, empty space. Scatter small hearts around the words (Robin’s love for her bees).

It works because the pages before it are quiet. It is the one big, still moment in a calm book, so keep it rare and gentle.

The Design System

What makes four dogs one family, and keeps each one clear even when tiny.

This system makes the four dogs look like one family, and keeps each dog clear even when printed very small (about two inches wide) on a toy tag.

One big idea per dog

Winston

Heavy and round

Maxi

A circle among circles

Angus

A single spike

Zuri

The night

Each dog has one big, simple idea, not many small details. A child sees each dog as one clear idea, the same way Peter Rabbit is a blue jacket or Clifford is red: one essential fact per character.

Angus has the strongest idea: no coat pattern, on purpose

Three dogs wear their magic in their coats. Angus refuses, and nobody explains why. This empty coat carries a lot of meaning, which is exactly what the best picture books do. But it is risky: his mohawk must be very clear, even bigger than feels comfortable. If it is too small, he will look like “the plain dog” by mistake.

The four colors

Winston

yellow

Maxi

pink

Angus

white

Zuri

blue

Pink, yellow, white, blue: four words every three-year-old already knows. A child needs to point at a dog and say “that one is me.” A child who chooses a favorite wants the book, then the toy, then the next book. No child is left out by a color they cannot name.

Why Zuri is the dark one

Three light dogs and one dark dog. Zuri is not a strange exception in a bright group, she is the same spring park, seen at midnight. The thing that makes her hard to see in daylight is the same thing that will give light in the dark, in a later book. The one who looks least like the others is not less, not strange, and not a problem to fix.

How the system helps the story

  • It sets up a joke thirty pages early. All four dogs have the same eye, seen about forty times. That is what makes Maxi’s huge eyes on page 20 funny. You cannot break a rule the reader has not learned yet.
  • It carries meaning with no words. Angus’s missing coat, Zuri’s midnight blue, Winston’s cloth instead of a flag, each says something true about belonging. The child feels it, without being told.

From Story to Action

The brand lives off the page. Every story ends in care, and the child can go and do it.

Every Green Paws story ends in care. In The Missing Bees, the Pack cares for the flowers at the end. Because the parks are real, a child can go and do the same thing, in the very park the dogs live in. The book is the spark. The park is where it comes to life.

The Green Paws park day

Children come to a real park and become the Pack for an afternoon. With their own Green Paws broom, they sniff, wag, and bark while they care for the park: noticing what is there, having fun, and sweeping the paths clean.

  • Sniff. Look closely. A noticing walk: find the small things on the path and in the grass.
  • Wag. Have fun. The joy of being outside with the Pack.
  • Bark. Care for it. Sweep the paths, tend the flowers, leave the park a little better.

Why it works: the tagline becomes something a child physically does, the book’s ending becomes the child’s own action, and the Green Paws broom lets a preschooler truly join the Pack. It is fun first, care second: the child sweeps because it is joyful, and the stewardship is the happy byproduct. Never a lesson, never a chore.

Why only Green Paws can do this: these park days are possible because the parks are real. An invented world cannot host a sweep day in a place that does not exist. The activation and the positioning are the same idea.

Partners who make it real: local parks commissioners, conservancies, and Friends-of-the-park groups host the days and keep each park true. They are place gatekeepers, alongside the reading gatekeepers (librarians, teachers, parents) who carry a quiet brand. Green Paws needs both, because it lives exactly where great children’s books meet real local parks.

Warmth Pulls It All Together

The rules are the skeleton. The warmth is the product.

All the rules above are careful discipline, the structure underneath. But none of it is the real point by itself. What makes this property lovable is not the silhouette test. It is Robin, sure she is starving while she is clearly plump. It is Winston, treating one missing bee as a real emergency. These are creatures who care about small things with complete sincerity, and that is where both the humor and the love come from.

A perfectly designed dog nobody loves is a failure. A slightly imperfect dog a child adores is the win.

A rule can guarantee a clean silhouette. It cannot guarantee that a child laughs, or reaches for the dog. That must be protected by hand, on every page. When elegance and warmth seem to conflict, warmth wins, and we solve the design around it.

One line to keep

You keep what makes you yourself, and you belong anyway.

This is the mission underneath the mission. The dogs already say it in how they are designed. A bulldog, a basenji, a poodle, and a terrier become one pack, not by becoming the same, but because they get to keep their key differences. Zuri, the quiet newcomer, is not made louder; she finds her own voice and solves what no one else could. Belonging is being included.
