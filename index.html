Green Paws Pack · Brand Strategy

The No-Screen
Strategy

Most children's brands begin on a screen. A child watches the show, then wants the toy. But three-to-five-year-olds don't need a screen to fall in love. They fall for a stuffed animal by holding it, and for a character by hearing the story read again and again. The stuffed animal and the story do the work a screen does for older children, and that changes the order of everything that follows.

Sequence: the proof-of-concept pitch book comes first, a private, not-for-sale edition made only for champions and connectors. Take it to a champion (Pleasant Rowland or Maxine Clark) and let the champion's name unlock a stronger publishing deal and a stronger plush deal in parallel. Then approach the park for a partnership.

Vision + mission

Vision · the world we want
Every child knows the magic of nature is theirs to love and care for.
Mission · what Green Paws does
Inspire children to look closely at, have fun with, and care for nature close to home.
The reasoning underneath

Looking closely, and gathering what you see, hear, smell, taste, or touch, is the root of early science thinking and critical thinking. Caring for what you find is the root of stewardship. The fun is what makes it stick. A child who enjoys looking closely and caring for a park will continue to do both.

02

GTM strategy

The proof-of-concept pitch book comes first
This is a private, not-for-sale edition, fully designed cover to cover with the key pieces illustrated, made only to show champions and connectors. A book someone can hold changes the conversation from "here is an idea" to "here is a property that exists," and it proves the thing every partner quietly worries about: that the creator finishes what they start. It is a pitch instrument, not a product.
Instead of chasing a publisher or a plush deal on the strength of a manuscript and a hope, which is the weakest possible negotiating position, with nothing to show.
The evidenceThe strongest precedent in the category did exactly this. After six publishers rejected her, Beatrix Potter privately printed small runs of The Tale of Peter Rabbit in 1901. The proof of demand persuaded Frederick Warne to publish it, and by Christmas 1902 it had sold roughly 20,000 copies. She then made the first Peter Rabbit doll and registered her designs, becoming the world's first licensed literary character. Prove small, own the rights, then let a partner scale it. Smithsonian Magazine, 2017; Penguin / Frederick Warne.
A champion to accelerate and sharpen the first deal
The plush and publishing worlds are gated, and a first-time creator with no sales history is easy to pass over. A champion's name collapses that. The point is not a bigger deal; it is a better version of the first, small deal, reached faster and on stronger terms. The champions chosen, Pleasant Rowland and Maxine Clark, both built properties by keeping control and aligning commerce with purpose, so their counsel is worth as much as their contacts.
Instead of an agent, which is slow, dilutive, and hands a stranger the very rights the whole property is built to retain.
The evidenceBoth champions built their own properties, Build-A-Bear and American Girl, by making products first and keeping control, so their counsel matches the strategy. The same Beatrix Potter history shows the cost of the alternative: her one failure to protect rights (the lost U.S. copyright) left her work pirated for years, while the trademark fortress built around the characters still guards them a century on. A champion's introduction keeps the rights conversation with the creator; an agent does not. Smithsonian Magazine, 2017; Frederick Warne trademark record.
Publishing and plush run in parallel, not in sequence
Both are unlocked by the same champion at the same moment, and neither has to wait for the other. Plush proves the characters in three dimensions and reaches the age directly; the retail book is the repetition engine a parent reads forty times. They reinforce each other, especially if the plush lands beside the retail book on the same shelf.
Instead of forcing an order between them and losing the momentum a champion's introduction creates while one waits on the other.
The evidenceBoth are large, growing categories that do different jobs. The global plush market was worth roughly $13.7B in 2025 and is growing, with dolls and plush the leading licensed-toy segment (about a fifth of licensed-toy sales) precisely for their emotional and character appeal, that is where the character is held and loved. The children's book market is large and steadily growing (about $12.4B in 2026), with the preschool segment a durable core, and the book is the thing a parent reads again and again. Plush creates the bond; the book repeats it. Grand View Research, 2026; Future Market Insights, 2025; The Business Research Company, 2026.
A plush partner that needs no screen to say yes
Every screen property uses toys and experiences to harvest fame it already has. This property has no screen and does not need one, because at three to five the toy in the hand and the story on the page are what make a child fall in love, no show required. That points to book-native, author-friendly houses, MerryMakers first, who take a property on the strength of the character rather than a streaming rank.
Instead of the volume giants who only move on properties with a show behind them, and would either pass or bury the Pack in a catalog of screen brands.
The evidenceParent anxiety about screens is high and rising: in a June 2025 survey, more than half of U.S. parents said they have felt their child is addicted to screens, and roughly seven in ten actively limit screen use; the AAP advises no screens under 18 months and about an hour a day for ages two to five. Meanwhile the toy industry uses experience at the end of the funnel, to harvest fame a property already built on a screen. Green Paws needs no screen because, at three to five, the object and the story are themselves how love forms, so it plants fame through presence instead. Lurie Children's, June 2025; AAP via CHOC, 2026; The Toy Book, 2026.
The park comes later, and gives back
Brooklyn Bridge Park is the real place the story is set, so making it a destination closes a loop no screen property can. But a public conservancy cannot sell branding of public land. Reframed as a gift, a floor per unit plus a share of profits pledged to the park's children's programming, it becomes a partnership they can say yes to. The more plush sells, the more the real park gets, and the more children come to it. Commerce funds the mission instead of trading against it.
Instead of leading with the park, which would mean negotiating the property's presentation with an institution that has no stake in its ownership, before there is leverage to protect it.
The reasoningA public conservancy cannot sell branding of public land, so a commercial ask fails on its face. Reframed as a gift, a guaranteed floor per unit plus a share of profits pledged to the park's children's programming, it becomes philanthropy the park can accept, and it quietly buys preferred standing without asking for exclusivity. The more plush sells, the more the park receives, and the more children come to it.

Note that the Green Paws park day is a benefit of the partnership, not a step toward it. Any organized activation in a public park requires a special event permit, and a permit application carrying the brand is itself the partnership conversation. The park day therefore cannot precede the partnership; it is one of the things the partnership makes possible.
Open item · park name use, pending counselThe park name is held, not settled. It does not appear in the manuscript, and it is deliberately not used publicly until confirmed. Naming a real place as a story's setting is expressive use and well protected, so the book is low risk. The real exposure is downstream: merchandise carrying the park's name, and a branded event held in the actual park, which shift toward commercial use that could imply an affiliation the property does not have.
The question for IP counsel (Thea Kerman) is not "can the park be named in a book," but at what point use of the name implies a partnership that does not exist, and what that means for merchandise and the park-day activation. Not urgent; it does not need resolving until plush and the park day are real, and should be raised alongside the ITU filings (Classes 16 and 41) and the toys/plush/apparel/pet-products expansion. Findings to date (July 29, 2026): no USPTO mark for "Brooklyn Bridge Park" surfaced under the park corporation, though that is not conclusive since US rights arise from use, not registration; Brooklyn Bridge Park Corporation is a NY public benefit corporation (members: the Mayor, First Deputy Mayor, and Deputy Mayor for Operations; 334 Furman Street, Brooklyn NY 11201), and public status does not preclude holding marks, as the Central Park Conservancy does. Throughout this document the park is named freely and the relationship treated as a 2028 item; that naming is held pending counsel.
The principle running through all of it: prove the property on a small scale, retain control, and let each stage of traction earn the next. No step depends on a deal that cannot be walked away from.

The book: publish or self

The proof-of-concept pitch book is a separate, earlier thing: a private edition made only to open doors. This section is about the retail book, the version actually sold, which comes after a champion. With a champion's name attached, the terms improve either way.

Self-publish first Recommended now

Produce and sell the retail edition yourself, owning everything and retaining all rights. This is the Beatrix Potter sequence: prove the property, then license from strength rather than giving rights away up front.

Strengths
  • Total control, all rights retained
  • Nothing to negotiate, no agent, no gate
  • Move at the creator's own pace, no publisher timeline
  • Keep the full margin on every copy sold
Weaknesses
  • No bookstore or library distribution
  • Self-published stigma with some gatekeepers
  • Print run self-funded

Trade publisher Later, from strength

A real imprint brings reach and legitimacy, but the standard contract acquires a book and takes rights. Better approached after a champion's name and a finished book change the terms in the creator's favor. This route deliberately avoids agents, which rules out the Big Five and most mid-size houses for a first approach.

Strengths
  • Bookstore and library distribution
  • Editorial and design credibility
  • Reach a self-published run can't match
Weaknesses
  • Most houses are agent-gated
  • World and merchandise rights at risk
  • 18–24 month timelines
  • Acquires a book, not a property
The distributed model is the destination, not the start. Care Bears splits publishing by channel — one house for trade, another for fairs and clubs. That only works once the property has value. Nobody licenses publishing rights by channel from an unknown creator with one unpublished book.
06

Publishing partners

Two routes, shown as two tiers. The first is independent houses that accept work without an agent, the faster and more control-friendly path. The second is major trade publishers, which generally require an agent, but where a champion's introduction can serve the same function for a property that already has a brand and traction behind it.

Two things to weigh in any deal, at either tier: whether the house takes a series or only a single title, and what happens to merchandise and subsidiary rights. For a property whose value is the franchise, rights retention outranks advance size.

Tier one · Independent, no agent required

The direct route. Slower reach than the giants, but accessible without representation and more willing to structure around a property.

  • Real school and library reach
Weaknesses

  • Smaller than the trade giants

Tier two · Trade publishers

The major houses. These generally require an agent for a first-time author, but that changes for a property that arrives with plush, a champion, and a proven brand. In that case a champion's introduction can do what an agent would, and keep the rights conversation in the creator's hands.

Plush partners

One of the two tracks the champion unlocks, alongside publishing. The Pack was designed to plush logic — the silhouette test, one big idea per dog, four nameable colors. The goal is a partner that needs no screen property to say yes, and a champion's name to sharpen the deal. The field splits cleanly, and only one side are buyers.

Two kinds of plush company. Original-design houses (Jellycat, Douglas) build only their own characters and license almost nothing — the aesthetic standard, not buyers. Licensing houses exist to take other people's IP to shelf. Only the second are candidates.
Start here

MerryMakers

Woman-owned · est. 1994 · Clair Frederick, pres.
Entire business is plush from children's picture books, made with the author and illustrator. Pete the Cat, Tiny T. Rex, The Very Hungry Caterpillar. Target merchandises their plush in the book department, next to the title.
Strengths
  • Book-native, so the property is their core case, not the odd one out
  • Fidelity to the illustration is their selling point
  • Sold beside the book, the ideal shelf
  • Likely a direct approach, no champion needed
Weaknesses
  • Specialty scale — lower volume ceiling
  • You will outgrow it; plan the exit into the entry
Premium heritage

Gund

Est. 1898 · owned by Spin Master
The premium book-plush house. Makes the official Mo Willems Pigeon and Elephant & Piggie in velvety under-stuffed style around $27. Heritage and department-store presence.
Strengths
  • Top-tier quality and prestige
  • Proven with book characters
  • Department-store reach
Weaknesses
  • Spin Master ownership — longer decision chain
  • Likely needs a champion to reach

The champion ask

The list is short and mostly mid-market, which is exactly why the champion matters. The highest-value thing a champion offers is not a company name — it is the answer to what makes a licensor say yes to a property with no sales history, and the door that name unlocks.

Who they are

Maxine Clark
Founder of Build-A-Bear Workshop, which built a business on children making and holding their own plush. Now an active mentor to women and minority entrepreneurs and vice chair of the PBS board. Plush and experiential retail are her native ground.
Pleasant Rowland
Founder of American Girl, which she built by making the products first and keeping control of the property rather than licensing it away. The authority on growing a character brand while retaining ownership.

The relationship we hope for

An advisor as the property grows. There may be only one meeting, so the aim is stated plainly rather than saved for a second conversation that may not come. Over time, the hope is help in four areas:

  1. Pressure-test the strategy. Tell us where the logic is wrong and where it is thin.

  2. Open doors. Use contacts to help bring on an IP lawyer and reach a plush partner.

  3. Weigh the book. Help decide between self-publishing and a book deal, open the right door for whichever, and advise on timing.

  4. Ongoing counsel. Continuing advice as the property grows.

Where each would start

Maxine Clark leads on plush and retail: whether the Pack works as plush, what is being missed on the retail and experience side, and an introduction to the right plush partner. Build-A-Bear is hers to raise.

Pleasant Rowland leads on control and publishing: how to structure the property so it stays owned outright, and whether to self-publish or place the book. She built American Girl by making the products first and keeping control, so that counsel is worth more than any single introduction.

Both are approached as kindred builders rather than as a pitch for money. The ask is belief, counsel, and doors. If either chooses to invest or fund, that is welcome, but it is their move to offer, not the reason for the meeting. The single most valuable question for either remains who else should be in the room.
